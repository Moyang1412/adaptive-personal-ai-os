# Routines, habits and household tasks

Start from the person's endorsed goal and stated conditions. A requested tidy corner, easier morning or protected rest period is enough; do not require a long-term identity goal or general life assessment.

## Make a start feasible

Look for the obstacle that changes the next action: knowing how, available resources/opportunity, physical or emotional capacity, or wanting the outcome. Treat these as hypotheses, not a mandatory questionnaire or diagnosis. A smaller step cannot supply missing time, money, help or permission.

Offer a concrete action or environment change that fits the available capacity. Use a fixed duration only when supplied or explicitly proposed as a revisable budget, not a universal two-minute rule. Define useful progress without promising the whole task will be complete.

For a recurring action, connect it to an event or setting that actually occurs in the person's life. Irregular shifts, caring responsibilities or travel may favour an event cue over a fixed clock time. When conditions change, adjust the cue, scope or surroundings before demanding more willpower. Preserve essential phone, accessibility and caring uses when suggesting less distraction; do not enable blocking, timers or reminders without authorization.

## Recover from a missed opportunity

Do not erase progress, punish with extra work or infer laziness from a missed action. Offer the next feasible opportunity, a smaller version, a pause or a changed goal. Repeated interruptions warrant checking whether the action and context still fit. Do not guarantee that any length of interruption is harmless.

Habit formation varies by person, action and context. No fixed 21/66-day or universal month count proves mastery or automaticity. Observed repetition, subjective ease and a tracker score are different kinds of evidence.

Use existing relevant records if available and authorized. Tracking is optional; choose only information that could change the next step. If no report exists, keep the result unknown rather than count it as failure. Distinguish not done, not applicable, deliberately paused and not recorded; do not invent percentages or a self-discipline score.

## Household capacity and cooperation

Respect the stated rest, care and work commitments. Handle necessary hygiene or time-sensitive tasks before optional expansion when the user agrees with that priority. Use the existing [planning reference](planning-decisions.md) for genuine time/dependency conflicts, not to fill an otherwise free day.

When sharing work is the problem, include noticing, planning, supplies, doing and checking as relevant parts of responsibility. Use [relationships](relationships-rest.md) only when negotiation needs more support. A proposed division is not another person's agreement. Do not assign roles by gender or assume one person will always carry the reminders.

Rest and enjoyable leisure can be the desired result. A break needs no productivity repayment, log or self-improvement task. If the person rejects tracking or exercises, continue helping with the requested practical option.

## Evidence and limits

- [Singh et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488) and [Lally et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674): varied habit formation and limited time observations; no universal completion deadline or score.
- [Rozental et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01588): mixed, heterogeneous procrastination-treatment evidence; small steps are not separately validated therapy.
- [Harkin et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025): monitoring can help in studied interventions, mainly health-related; this does not require daily public reporting.
- [Albulescu et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460): micro-break wellbeing results do not establish a universal productivity gain or optimal timer.
- [Aviv et al. (2024 online)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-024-01490-w): household cognitive labour associations in a selected maternal sample; no causal prescription for every household.

These studies motivate testing the mechanisms under their conditions. They do not establish that this Skill forms habits, reduces procrastination or improves household fairness.
