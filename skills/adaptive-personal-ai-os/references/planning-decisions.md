# Planning and decisions

Use this reference when a plan or choice needs more than a direct answer. It is a research-informed protocol, not evidence that ZhiXu improves decisions or productivity.

## Match the task

Start from the stated goal and already available context. Give useful help immediately when the need is clear; do not run a general intake interview first.

Scale detail to consequences, uncertainty and reversibility. A routine choice may need one recommendation and its reason. A consequential choice may need explicit alternatives, evidence and conditions. Do not force every field below into the response.

Ask only for a missing fact or preference that could change the next action. When a safe assumption suffices, label it and make it easy to correct. Do not infer unseen commitments, resources or calendar availability.

## Build an executable plan

- **Capacity and dependencies:** use known time, energy, tools, obligations and dependencies. Protect stated rest and commitments. When capacity is unknown, offer a provisional scope instead of pretending that a full schedule fits.
- **Completion condition:** describe an observable result at the needed quality. Distinguish a useful draft, a finished deliverable and an externally confirmed outcome.
- **Next action:** identify one concrete action the person can start with available resources. Add later steps only when they help coordinate dependencies or reveal a material risk.
- **Insufficient capacity:** reduce scope, move an optional task, obtain a missing resource or reconsider the deadline. Do not respond only by demanding more effort or inventing extra available hours.

When remembering, starting or handling a recurring obstacle is the problem, propose a precise cue-to-action link: “If [recognizable situation], then [feasible action].” Add an obstacle alternative only when useful. The cue must plausibly occur and the action must serve a goal the user endorses; incompatible actions must not share the same cue.

A cue-to-action plan cannot supply missing skill, money, permission or time. A suggested time is a proposal, not a calendar booking. Do not create reminders, recurring jobs or external commitments without the authorization and tools required by the host.

For duration estimates, first reuse relevant actual records when available. Check whether scope, conditions and dependencies are comparable. State a useful range and its assumptions rather than unsupported precision. Without comparable records, label the estimate provisional; do not invent a historical average or silently apply a universal multiplier.

Preserve the meaning of numerical inputs when combining them: a lower bound is not an exact estimate or an upper bound. If a task has no known upper duration, the total has no supported finite upper bound unless a separate cap is stated as an assumption.

When outcomes become available, compare estimated and actual duration and investigate material differences. More conservative estimates are not necessarily more accurate. Do not require new tracking or persistence for a simple request.

## Make the choice inspectable

1. Apply hard constraints before ranking genuine options. Include keeping the current state or deferring only when feasible and relevant; neither is automatically harmless.
2. Separate **facts** supported by evidence, **estimates** with uncertainty, and **values** supplied by the user. Do not turn a preference into a fact or invent weights, scores or probabilities to produce a ranking.
3. Compare the differences that matter: likely benefit, total effort or cost, downside, dependencies and reversibility. Use the user's criteria; if a missing preference changes the recommendation, expose that tradeoff or ask one targeted question.
4. Identify the key unknown that could change the choice. Search or ask only when the answer could matter enough to justify its cost, time and delay. More information is not always better.
5. Check sensitivity: explain which plausible change in an assumption or priority would reverse the recommendation. Use qualitative conditions when numerical inputs are unsupported. If a conclusion is fragile, say so.

When a low-cost, reversible trial can resolve a key unknown, propose it with:

- the uncertainty to resolve and the decision it informs;
- a bounded scope, cost or duration appropriate to the situation;
- an observable result that would favor continuing, changing or stopping;
- a stopping condition and any necessary rollback or exit path.

A trial is optional, not a prerequisite to every choice. Do not delay an urgent decision or essential safety action to collect more evidence. Do not frame risky, irreversible or high-stakes actions as casual self-experiments.

Stop analysis when there is enough information for the next decision: give the recommendation, the main reason and the material uncertainty or change condition. If one unresolved condition prevents a responsible choice, name it and provide a useful interim action. Do not continue searching merely to fill a comparison table.

## Short fictional example

Lena has 25 minutes, rough notes and a request for a one-page meeting brief. Propose a draft containing the decision needed and two supporting facts, then reserve the remaining time for a check. If she is interrupted, preserve the key decision and mark the missing evidence rather than claiming the brief is complete. This is an invented illustration, not a user case or an efficacy result.

## Evidence and limits

- **Implementation intentions:** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), DOI [10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1), [full text from the authors' institution](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/06_Gollwitzer_Sheeran_Implementation_Intentions_And_Goal.pdf). The meta-analysis supports linking cues with goal-directed actions, with substantial variation and many student samples. It does not validate AI-generated plans, replace resources or justify a guaranteed improvement percentage.
- **Planning fallacy:** Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), DOI [10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366), [full text](https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Exploring-the-Planning-Fallacy_Why-People-Underestimate-Their-Task-Completion-Times.pdf). Connecting relevant past experience to forecasts reduced optimistic bias in one study; the paper explicitly distinguishes this from improved absolute accuracy. Recalled history can be wrong or incomparable.
- **Engineering reference:** [LifeOS Algorithm 8.20.2, pinned source](https://github.com/danielmiessler/LifeOS/blob/5e2f2e8c0abde612da0e99c16c0d07d4ec21b88c/LifeOS/install/LIFEOS/ALGORITHM/v8.20.2.md), inspected 2026-10-13. Borrow observable completion criteria and evidence matched to claims, not its full infrastructure or required artifacts for every task. Repository design is not scientific efficacy evidence.

Capacity checks, selective information gathering, sensitivity checks and bounded trials here are design choices informed by these distinctions, not a validated intervention package. Evaluate usefulness, completion quality, decision errors and user burden in actual use; fewer questions or a passing synthetic dialogue alone does not prove better outcomes.
